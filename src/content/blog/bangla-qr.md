## 1. What Is Bangla QR, and Why Does It Matter?

Bangla QR is Bangladesh's national standard for QR-code-based retail payments, developed by Bangladesh Bank to make payments more interoperable across participating banks, mobile financial services (MFS), and other payment providers.

Under this system, a merchant displays a QR code, and a customer scans it using a compatible financial institution's application to make a payment. Depending on the implementation, the customer may enter the amount manually or scan a code that already contains the payment amount.

The central idea is **interoperability**: customers should not have to navigate a fragmented collection of payment systems simply to pay a merchant. Bangladesh Bank's guidelines identify low-cost payments, broader merchant participation, and an interoperable payment ecosystem as important objectives. [Source: Bangladesh Bank's Bangla QR guidelines](https://www.bb.org.bd/mediaroom/circulars/psd/jan062021psd01.pdf).

Consider a small grocery shop in Dhaka. The owner may not be able to justify purchasing a traditional point-of-sale terminal. A QR code could offer a comparatively inexpensive way to accept digital payments. If customers can pay using the financial applications they already use, the shop could become part of the formal digital payment ecosystem without investing in expensive equipment.

But there is a distinction worth remembering: **making a payment method available is not the same as making it widely used.**

A merchant may display a QR code while most customers continue paying in cash. A customer may have a digital wallet but prefer cash for small purchases. The economic impact depends on what happens after the technology is introduced.

## 2. What Do the Numbers Tell Us About Bangladesh?

Bangladesh has already developed a substantial digital financial services ecosystem. Nevertheless, access to financial accounts, access to smartphones, and the regular use of interoperable digital payments are different things.

The World Bank's [Digital Finance country profile for Bangladesh](https://digitalfinance.worldbank.org/country/bangladesh) provides indicators on account ownership, digital payments, and differences across population groups. These indicators are useful for understanding the broader environment in which Bangla QR operates.

More directly, Bangladesh Bank's Annual Report for FY2024–25 provides a revealing snapshot of Bangla QR transactions processed through the National Payment Switch Bangladesh (NPSB).

| Indicator | FY2024–25 |
|---|---:|
| Bangla QR transactions settled through NPSB | Approximately 4.165 million |
| Transaction value | Approximately Tk 20.516 billion |
| Change in transaction volume from FY2023–24 | −11.00% |
| Change in transaction value from FY2023–24 | +19.64% |

Source: [Bangladesh Bank, Annual Report 2024–25](https://www.bb.org.bd/pub/annual/anreport/ar2024-2025.pdf).

These figures deserve closer examination.

### An interesting economic puzzle

Transaction volume declined by 11%, while transaction value increased by approximately 19.64%.

What might explain this difference?

One possibility is that the average transaction became larger. Another is that the mix of merchants, customers, or transactions changed. Changes in the reporting or routing of transactions may also matter.

The figures alone do not establish which explanation is correct.

**Interactive reflection:** Imagine you are advising Bangladesh Bank. Would you judge Bangla QR's performance primarily by the total value of transactions or by the number of payments made?

The better answer is to examine both, alongside active merchants, repeat users, transaction failures, costs, and the distribution of adoption across urban and rural areas.

There is also an important limitation: these figures cover transactions settled through NPSB, not necessarily every Bangla QR transaction across all payment networks. They should not be interpreted as a complete measure of national QR usage.

The figures are also historical, covering FY2024–25. They provide a baseline, not a measurement of the system's current performance.

## 3. International Lessons: What Can Bangladesh Learn?

Bangladesh is not the first country to explore the economic potential of interoperable digital payments. India, Thailand, and Brazil demonstrate three different aspects of the transformation: widespread payment acceptance, payment-network interoperability, and the development of public digital payment infrastructure.

### India: UPI and the network effect

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) connects participating banks and payment applications through a common real-time payment infrastructure.

According to India's Press Information Bureau, UPI accounted for **81% of retail digital payment transactions in FY2024–25**. Total retail digital payment transactions reached 22,167.90 crore during that financial year, with UPI accounting for the dominant share. [Source: Government of India, 16 March 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2240723&lang=1&reg=1).

India's experience illustrates an important economic concept: the **network effect**.

A payment system becomes more useful as more customers and merchants accept it. More merchants encourage customers to adopt the system, while a larger customer base gives merchants a stronger reason to accept it.

For Bangladesh, the lesson is not simply to distribute more QR stickers. It is to make the system consistently useful across the places where people already shop.

Imagine a customer who can pay digitally at a supermarket but must use cash at a neighborhood tea stall, a roadside vegetable seller, or a local transport provider. Digital payments remain inconvenient if acceptance is fragmented.

The more widely interoperable payments work across everyday transactions, the greater their potential value.

However, India's UPI and Bangladesh's Bangla QR are not identical systems. UPI is a broader instant-payment interface, while Bangla QR is a national QR-payment standard. Their transaction statistics therefore cannot be directly compared as though they measured the same thing.

**Lesson for Bangladesh:** Build a payment ecosystem that customers can use repeatedly, rather than measuring success only by the number of merchants registered.

### Thailand: PromptPay and the importance of interoperability

Thailand's PromptPay system links payments to identifiers such as phone numbers and other registered details, while Thailand's QR-payment ecosystem supports retail transactions.

In a May 2024 article, the Bank of Thailand reported approximately **77 million registered PromptPay identifiers and around 61 million transfers per day**. These figures reflect the system's adoption at that time, not its current daily volume. [Source: Bank of Thailand](https://www.bot.or.th/th/research-and-publications/articles-and-publications/articles/article-2024may14.html).

Thailand's experience highlights how digital payments can become part of routine economic activity rather than a service used only by technologically advanced businesses.

It also demonstrates the potential of interoperability beyond domestic transactions. Thailand has developed QR-payment connections with several other economies, supporting cross-border payments for tourism and other activities.

Bangladesh could eventually explore similar opportunities with regional partners, subject to regulatory agreements, exchange-rate arrangements, settlement infrastructure, and consumer protections.

For a Bangladeshi visitor in another country, a well-designed cross-border payment arrangement could reduce the need to exchange cash. For foreign visitors in Bangladesh, it could potentially simplify payments at hotels, restaurants, shops, and tourist destinations.

These benefits would require specific agreements and implementation; they should not be assumed to exist simply because Bangla QR is available domestically.

**Lesson for Bangladesh:** Interoperability can create value beyond convenience. It can also support tourism, cross-border commerce, and more connected payment infrastructure.

### Brazil: Pix and the power of public payment infrastructure

Brazil's Pix system, launched by the Central Bank of Brazil in 2020, enables rapid electronic transfers between individuals and businesses. QR codes are one of several ways to initiate Pix payments.

The Central Bank of Brazil's official statistics reported that, in January 2026, Pix recorded **more than 7 billion transactions in a month** and had been used by more than 170 million individual users, equivalent to roughly 80% of the population. [Source: Central Bank of Brazil, Pix statistics](https://www.bcb.gov.br/estabilidadefinanceira/pix-em-numeros-estatisticas).

Pix illustrates the scale that a broadly accessible payment infrastructure can reach. It also shows why payment design matters: convenience, wide acceptance, clear rules, and a reliable system can encourage repeated use.

However, those statistics do not mean that Pix alone caused Brazil's broader economic development, nor do they prove that introducing a similar system would produce identical results in Bangladesh. The two economies differ in infrastructure, institutions, market conditions, and financial behavior.

**Lesson for Bangladesh:** A national payment system can become economically important when it is reliable and useful enough to become part of everyday life.

### Three countries, three lessons

| Country | What stands out | Potential lesson for Bangladesh |
|---|---|---|
| India | UPI's scale and broad digital-payment use | Build network effects and everyday merchant acceptance |
| Thailand | PromptPay and connected QR-payment infrastructure | Improve interoperability and explore regional connections |
| Brazil | Pix's widespread use | Prioritize reliability, accessibility, and simple payment experiences |

The common theme is not QR technology alone. It is the combination of shared infrastructure, convenient access, trusted institutions, and sustained adoption.

## 4. How Could Bangla QR Change Bangladesh's Economy?

The potential economic impact extends beyond the payment itself. It could influence business costs, financial inclusion, recordkeeping, consumer behavior, and the way financial institutions compete.

### 4.1 Lower transaction costs for small businesses

Cash transactions have costs that are easy to overlook.

Business owners may need to obtain change, count cash, travel to deposit money, reconcile daily sales, and manage the risks associated with holding physical money. Digital payments may reduce some of these burdens.

QR codes can also be cheaper to deploy than conventional card-payment terminals, making them potentially attractive to micro and small businesses.

Consider a hypothetical shop that processes 50 digital payments per day. If improved payment procedures save an average of 20 seconds per transaction, the shop saves approximately 16.7 minutes of transaction-handling time daily.

This is an illustrative calculation, not an observed result of Bangla QR. Actual savings would depend on payment speed, network reliability, customer behavior, and the time required to confirm transactions.

There is another important consideration: payment-provider charges, settlement delays, failed transactions, and reconciliation problems can offset some of the savings.

**Economic implication:** The relevant question is not whether QR payments are modern, but whether their total cost to a merchant is lower than the cost of the alternatives.

### 4.2 Greater financial inclusion

Financial inclusion means more than opening bank accounts. It includes the ability to use suitable financial services safely, affordably, and consistently.

Bangla QR could make digital payments more accessible to merchants who cannot justify expensive payment equipment. It may also allow customers to make payments through participating financial applications they already use.

Potential beneficiaries include:

- Small retailers and neighborhood businesses.
- Women-owned microenterprises.
- Rural merchants and service providers.
- Customers who increasingly use digital financial services.
- Tourism-related businesses that serve domestic and international visitors.

Yet digital payments cannot automatically include everyone.

People without suitable phones, reliable connectivity, compatible accounts, or the skills to use digital applications may remain excluded. Some may depend on family members or intermediaries, creating additional privacy and security concerns.

**Economic implication:** A QR system is inclusive only when the people most likely to be excluded can use it in practice.

### 4.3 Better business records and access to finance

Digital payments can create a transaction history that helps a business understand its sales patterns, cash flow, and customer activity.

Suppose a small shop receives most payments in cash. Its sales records may be incomplete, making it harder to demonstrate regular business income when applying for formal credit.

If the same shop receives a meaningful share of its sales digitally, its payment records may provide additional evidence of commercial activity.

Lenders could potentially use such information, with appropriate consent and safeguards, when assessing creditworthiness.

But there is a crucial distinction: **a digital payment record is not the same as proof of profit or creditworthiness.**

Revenue does not reveal all operating costs, outstanding debts, seasonal risks, or household withdrawals. A business may also have significant cash sales that are not represented in its digital records.

For digital transaction histories to improve access to credit, financial institutions need responsible data-use policies, suitable credit-assessment models, and mechanisms to prevent unfair discrimination.

### 4.4 Greater transparency and formalization

Digital transactions can generate records that help businesses reconcile accounts and can support better financial reporting.

Over time, this may encourage some businesses to adopt more formal accounting practices.

Better records may also help firms understand demand, manage inventory, and identify seasonal changes in sales.

Nevertheless, increased digital visibility does not automatically translate into higher productivity, greater tax compliance, or improved government revenue. These outcomes depend on how businesses use the information and how public institutions design their policies.

A successful policy should encourage formalization without imposing disproportionate compliance costs on small enterprises.

### 4.5 Changes in consumer behavior

Payment methods can influence how people shop.

Digital payments reduce the need to carry cash and may make transactions feel quicker. However, consumers may also spend differently when payments become more convenient.

For example, a customer may find it easier to make several small purchases when paying digitally. Whether this leads to higher total spending depends on individual behavior, the type of purchase, and other circumstances.

There are also practical barriers. A customer may hesitate to use QR payments because of connectivity problems, uncertainty about the recipient, concerns about fraud, or a preference for cash.

This makes consumer trust a central economic issue.

A payment system that is technically functional but perceived as unreliable may struggle to achieve widespread adoption.

## 5. The Challenges: What Could Prevent Success?

A balanced assessment must examine the risks alongside the potential benefits.

**First, merchant adoption is not the same as active usage.** A registered merchant may rarely receive QR payments. Measuring active merchants and repeat transactions would provide a more meaningful assessment of progress.

**Second, payment reliability matters.** Slow confirmation, network interruptions, failed transfers, and unclear settlement timelines can discourage both merchants and customers.

**Third, charges must remain transparent.** Bangladesh Bank's Bangla QR guidelines address merchant discount rates and other fees, including the principle that the merchant discount rate should not be passed on to customers. The actual fee structure applicable to different participants and payment routes should be checked against the relevant current rules.

**Fourth, fraud prevention must keep pace with adoption.** Customers need to verify the merchant's identity before confirming a payment. Providers need clear procedures for reporting suspicious transactions, resolving disputes, and communicating payment status.

**Fifth, digital exclusion remains a real risk.** People who lack devices, connectivity, digital skills, or accessible interfaces may struggle to participate.

Finally, competition and interoperability require continued attention. A common QR standard can reduce fragmentation, but the system still needs consistent technical implementation, fair access, effective oversight, and reliable dispute resolution.

## 6. What Could the Future Look Like?

The future impact of Bangla QR will depend on policy choices and the way businesses and consumers respond. Three scenarios illustrate the possibilities.

### Scenario A: Limited adoption

Merchants display QR codes, but customers continue using cash for most everyday purchases. Digital transactions remain concentrated among larger retailers, urban customers, and people who are already comfortable with digital finance.

In this scenario, the system offers convenience to some users but produces limited economy-wide change.

### Scenario B: Broad domestic adoption

More small businesses accept interoperable QR payments, customers use them regularly, and providers improve reliability and dispute resolution.

Businesses may benefit from better transaction records and reduced cash-handling burdens. Customers may gain more convenient payment options, while payment providers compete to offer useful services.

This scenario could contribute to financial inclusion and business efficiency, although the size of those benefits would need to be measured rather than assumed.

### Scenario C: An integrated digital payment ecosystem

Bangla QR becomes part of a broader infrastructure that connects retail payments with accounting tools, digital invoicing, suitable credit products, government services, and potentially cross-border payment arrangements.

In this scenario, the benefits could extend beyond individual transactions to business management, tourism, financial innovation, and more efficient service delivery.

However, the broader the ecosystem becomes, the more important data privacy, cybersecurity, competition, and consumer protection will be.

These are possible pathways, not forecasts. Actual outcomes will depend on adoption, implementation quality, and the wider economic environment.

## 7. How Can Bangladesh Maximize the Economic Impact?

Bangladesh should evaluate Bangla QR as both a payment technology and an economic policy initiative.

**1. Measure real usage, not just registration.** Publish regular statistics on active merchants, repeat customers, transaction frequency, transaction values, failed payments, and geographic distribution.

**2. Reduce adoption barriers for small merchants.** Provide clear onboarding procedures, accessible training, transparent fees, and practical support for resolving technical problems.

**3. Strengthen trust and consumer protection.** Make recipient verification, payment confirmation, complaint handling, and dispute resolution straightforward.

**4. Expand access without excluding cash users.** Digital payments should increase choice rather than make essential transactions inaccessible to people who cannot use digital services.

**5. Support responsible use of transaction data.** Encourage useful accounting and financial services while protecting privacy and preventing unfair credit decisions.

**6. Learn from international experience without copying it blindly.** India, Thailand, and Brazil offer useful examples, but Bangladesh must adapt lessons to its own financial institutions, consumer preferences, infrastructure, and business environment.

**7. Evaluate the policy rigorously.** Compare businesses with different levels of adoption, examine changes over time, and investigate whether QR usage is associated with lower costs, improved recordkeeping, or greater access to formal finance.

Where possible, researchers should distinguish correlation from causation. Businesses that adopt digital payments may already be larger, more productive, or more connected than those that do not.

## 8. How Should We Measure Whether Bangla QR Is Working?

A useful evaluation framework would combine payment statistics with evidence about the economic experiences of merchants and customers.

| Indicator | What it would tell us |
|---|---|
| Active merchants | Whether registered businesses actually use the system |
| Transactions per active merchant | How frequently merchants receive QR payments |
| Average transaction value | Whether QR is being used for small purchases or larger transactions |
| Failed-payment rate | Whether the system is reliable |
| Merchant payment costs | Whether QR reduces total transaction costs |
| Rural and urban adoption | Whether access is becoming more inclusive |
| Small-business recordkeeping | Whether digital payments improve financial management |
| Access to formal credit | Whether useful transaction records translate into better financial opportunities |
| Consumer complaints and fraud | Whether growth is accompanied by adequate protection |

A stronger research design could compare outcomes before and after adoption, while accounting for differences between businesses. Where suitable data are available, researchers could also investigate differences in adoption across regions or examine policy changes that affect some merchants more than others.

Such analysis would help answer a more important question than how many QR codes have been distributed: **Are businesses and consumers actually better off because of the system?**

## Conclusion: Beyond the QR Code

Bangla QR represents an opportunity to modernize Bangladesh's payment infrastructure and make digital transactions more accessible to businesses and consumers.

International experiences show that interoperable payment systems can achieve substantial scale. India's UPI demonstrates the importance of network effects, Thailand's PromptPay highlights the value of connected payment infrastructure, and Brazil's Pix illustrates how a national payment system can become part of everyday economic activity.

For Bangladesh, the potential benefits include lower cash-handling burdens, more convenient transactions, improved business records, and greater participation in the digital financial ecosystem.

But none of these outcomes is guaranteed by technology alone.

The real test is whether small businesses can use Bangla QR affordably, whether customers trust it, whether transactions work reliably, and whether the resulting data and infrastructure improve economic opportunities.

Bangladesh should therefore measure the success of Bangla QR not simply by how many merchants display a code, but by how effectively the system serves people who transact, trade, save, and earn their livelihoods every day.

**The future of digital payments will not be determined by the QR code itself. It will be determined by the economic value that people can reliably create through it.**
