Artificial intelligence is changing the way businesses operate. From customer service and data analysis to manufacturing and administrative work, companies are increasingly exploring whether tasks traditionally performed by people can be completed by software, algorithms, and automated systems.

This shift is often explained as the natural consequence of technological progress. AI can process information quickly, operate continuously, and perform certain tasks at a lower cost. But there is another part of the story that deserves greater attention: **the tax system can influence whether companies find it more financially attractive to invest in technology or hire human workers.**

If tax policies make investment in automation relatively cheaper than employing people, businesses may have an additional financial incentive to replace workers—even when the productivity benefits of doing so are limited.

The question, therefore, is not simply whether AI can replace human labor. It is whether public policy is shaping the economic incentives behind that decision.

## The Unequal Tax Treatment of Labor and Capital

Businesses make hiring and investment decisions by comparing expected costs and benefits. Employing a worker involves wages, payroll taxes, social security contributions, and other employment-related expenses. Investing in software or equipment involves purchase costs, maintenance, financing, and applicable taxes.

Tax rules can change the balance between these options.

A 2020 study by Daron Acemoglu, Andrea Manera, and Pascual Restrepo, published in *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity*, examined how the United States tax code treats labor and capital. The researchers found that effective taxes on labor were substantially higher than those on investments in software and equipment.

Their analysis estimated effective tax rates on labor in the range of 25.5% to 33.5%, compared with approximately 5% on software and equipment following the US tax reforms enacted in 2017. These are estimates based on the study's methodology and historical US tax rules, not universal rates applicable to every company or country.

One important factor was the tax treatment of depreciation. Businesses may be allowed to deduct the cost of qualifying equipment and software from taxable income, sometimes much earlier than the assets' useful lives would otherwise suggest. Such provisions reduce the after-tax cost of investing in eligible capital.

When the tax system places a relatively heavier burden on employment while offering favorable treatment to capital investment, automation can become more attractive for financial reasons—not only because it improves productivity.

[Read the research: Acemoglu, Manera, and Restrepo (2020)](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/does-the-u-s-tax-code-favor-automation/)

## When Tax Incentives Encourage Too Much Automation

Automation is not inherently harmful to workers. Machines can improve safety, reduce repetitive work, increase output, and help businesses develop new products. AI can also complement human expertise, allowing employees to perform tasks more effectively.

The problem arises when companies automate tasks primarily because the tax system makes automation financially attractive, even when the resulting productivity gains are relatively small.

Acemoglu, Manera, and Restrepo describe this problem through the concept of excessive automation. In their model, the existing tax imbalance encourages firms to automate more tasks and use less labor than would be socially desirable.

Their analysis estimated that moving toward a more balanced tax system could increase employment by around 4% under the model's assumptions. They also examined an automation tax designed to discourage substitution of workers where the productivity gains are insufficient.

These results are model-based estimates, not guarantees that a particular tax reform would produce the same employment gains in today's AI economy.

Nevertheless, the underlying economic question is important: should tax policy encourage a company to replace a worker when the technology offers only a marginal improvement in productivity?

A business may reasonably pursue lower costs. But the private return from an investment does not always capture its broader social consequences, including unemployment risks, lost worker income, and the costs of transitioning into new occupations.

## The Debate Over an Automation Tax

One proposed response is an *automation tax*—a policy intended to reduce the tax advantage of replacing human labor with machines.

The purpose would not necessarily be to tax every AI system or discourage technological innovation. Instead, policymakers could consider whether existing tax rules unintentionally favor labor-replacing investment over technologies that help workers become more productive.

However, a blanket tax on AI or robots raises difficult questions.

How should governments define automation? Should a company pay additional tax for software that assists employees, as well as for software that eliminates positions? Could an automation tax discourage useful innovation, reduce investment, or make businesses less competitive?

These concerns matter because technological change can also create new jobs and industries.

A Brookings analysis, [“Tax Not the Robots”](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/tax-not-the-robots/), discusses some of the challenges associated with taxing robots directly. It considers alternatives such as reforming the tax treatment of capital and labor and using broader labor-market policies to support displaced workers.

The policy objective, therefore, need not be to stop automation. It can instead be to remove artificial incentives that distort investment decisions while helping workers adapt to technological change.

## Who Benefits From AI-Driven Productivity Growth?

The tax debate also raises a broader question about economic inequality: who receives the benefits when technology makes production more efficient?

If AI enables a company to produce the same output with fewer employees, its operating costs may fall and its profits may rise. Yet the distribution of those gains depends on wages, competition, ownership, and the company's decisions about investment and employment.

Workers may benefit when AI complements their skills, raises productivity, and creates opportunities for better-paid work. They may face greater uncertainty when technology replaces tasks they previously performed and suitable alternative jobs are unavailable.

The OECD's *Employment Outlook 2023* emphasizes that the effects of AI on employment are complex. AI can substitute for labor in some tasks while complementing workers in others. The OECD also highlights the importance of education, training, social dialogue, and reviewing the balance between labor and capital taxation.

[Source: OECD Employment Outlook 2023](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en/full-report/artificial-intelligence-and-jobs-no-signs-of-slowing-labour-demand-yet_5aebe670.html)

This distinction is crucial. The objective should not be to preserve every existing task indefinitely, but to ensure that technological progress does not systematically disadvantage workers or concentrate its benefits among those who own the technology.

## What Should Governments Do?

There is no single tax reform that will resolve every challenge associated with AI. However, several policy options deserve consideration.

**First, review the tax treatment of labor and capital.** Governments can assess whether differences in payroll taxes, depreciation allowances, and other provisions create unnecessary incentives to replace workers.

**Second, encourage worker-complementing innovation.** Tax incentives, public research funding, and business support could be designed to encourage technologies that improve human productivity rather than focusing exclusively on replacing human tasks.

**Third, invest in education and retraining.** Workers need accessible opportunities to develop skills relevant to changing labor markets. Training programs should be connected to actual employment opportunities rather than treating retraining as a universal solution to displacement.

**Fourth, strengthen social protection.** Unemployment support, employment services, and effective transition programs can help workers manage the costs of technological change.

**Finally, evaluate automation taxes carefully.** Policymakers should compare their potential employment benefits with their effects on investment, innovation, productivity, and government revenue. Reforming existing tax distortions may sometimes be more effective than imposing a separate tax on AI systems.

The International Labour Organization's research on generative AI similarly emphasizes the importance of policies that support job quality and a fair transition.

[Source: ILO, *Generative AI and Jobs: Policies to Manage the Transition* (2023)](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-policies-manage-transition)

## Conclusion: The Future of Work Is Also a Question of Tax Policy

The debate over AI and employment is often framed as a contest between human capability and technological progress. But this framing overlooks the institutions and economic incentives that shape how technology is adopted.

Companies respond not only to technological possibilities but also to wages, taxes, investment costs, and government policy. When tax rules favor capital investment over employment, they can influence the direction of technological change.

This does not mean that AI should be taxed simply because it can perform human tasks. Nor does it mean that every automated job would otherwise remain economically viable. The effects of taxation depend on the policy design, the industry, and the broader economic environment.

The more fundamental question is whether tax systems are appropriately balancing productivity, employment, innovation, and social welfare.

**If governments want technological progress to benefit society broadly, they must examine not only what AI can do, but also what their economic policies encourage businesses to do with it.**

The future of work will be shaped by technological innovation—but also by the public policies that determine who benefits from that innovation.
